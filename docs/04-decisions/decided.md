| D48 | Sound is a FOURTH data layer: a **sound pack** (files + cue/bed vocabulary, git-versioned like a component pack) is the menu; the **theme** gains `sound` (which cue for which entrance, mood→bed, the gain numbers); the **style pack** gains `sfx`/`music` (how often, how long); the **channel** keeps the master on/off and a trim. Cues and beds are placed by the COMPILER as concrete `sfx[]`/`music[]` items, never resolved at render time | Sound is a look, and the existing three-layer table already says where a look lives — inventing a fifth concept would have been the expensive answer to a question the table already answers. Compile-time placement is the load-bearing choice: a cue in the plan is addressable by the editor, survives a per-beat recompile under the D14 lock rules, and mixes identically on both render paths; a cue resolved inside the Remotion composition would be none of those and the ffmpeg path could not see it at all. The alternative — nesting a `sound` field on `overlayItem` — was rejected for the same reasons: invisible on the timeline, unmovable on its own, and duplicated in two renderers |
| D49 | Ducking is a **computed `gain_envelope`** on each music item — piecewise-linear absolute-time points the compiler derives from the real per-sentence TTS timings — not a sidechain compressor. Plus one `loudnorm` pass to -14 LUFS on the finished mix, on both paths | The timings are already exact, so the duck is arithmetic, and Principle 3 says code does arithmetic. A compressor would be nondeterministic, would sound different on the two render paths, would be invisible in the plan and uneditable in the UI. -14 LUFS is YouTube's target, so delivering at it means the platform's own normalization leaves the mix alone — most of the perceptual gap between "auto-generated" and "produced". The theme's `music_duck`/`music_lift` are ABSOLUTE levels and the channel's `default_volume` is a trim: two absolute levels multiplied together is what buries a bed 40 dB under the voice |
| D50 | The LLM never names a sound. Its whole contribution is `mood` per beat (a free string, unknown values degrading to `neutral`); the compiler groups contiguous same-mood beats into spans with a minimum length, and the theme maps mood→bed | Sound selection is consistent taste — exactly what D8 removed from the model's job — and a cue menu in every planner prompt would cost tokens on every call. `mood` was already in the beat sheet, already written by the planner, and read by nothing; this gives it its first consumer. Degrading rather than failing because a planner writing "ominous" instead of "tense" has made a reasonable word choice, and failing a whole video over vocabulary would be absurd. The span floor exists because a two-beat mood blip would otherwise restart the bed and the score would read as a mistake |
| D51 | Per-beat hold bounds are style-pack DATA (`pacing.hold_floor_ratio` / `hold_ceiling_ratio`, both defaulting to 0 = off, shipped packs at 1.0 / 1.5), enforced by the compiler after alignment and re-checked by `validate_plan`; a sub-floor beat MERGES into its neighbour's visual slot without rewriting the beat sheet, and an over-ceiling slot is divided into equal slots | Beat duration is the one pacing number nobody authors: the planner writes a span of script and the SRT decides how long it takes to say, so `min_hold`/`max_hold` were advice about a number that emerged after the fact — beats landed at 0.8s (a flash) and at 14s (one long sentence, no boundary to cut at, dead air). Merging at the VISUAL-slot level rather than in `beats.json` is the load-bearing choice: the beat sheet stays the validated artifact whose verbatim, ordered, gap-free coverage of the script is the whole contract, so the absorbed beat still owns its overlay and its mood and only a CUT moved. Ratios rather than absolute seconds because the numbers must stay consistent with the min/max they qualify; 0 as the schema default because a style pack is snapshotted at enqueue and an in-flight or re-run video must compile byte-identically (Principle 7); `locked` items exempt from the validator because a human who dragged a cut has made a decision that outranks the pack (D39) |
| D52 | Long scripts are planned in CHUNKS (`planner.chunk_target_beats`, default 30), and WHERE the chunks are cut is chosen by a cheap **spine** pass — a fourth prompt role but still the second agent: one call over the whole script returning section boundaries as sentence INDICES plus a one-line summary each, sharing `planner.llm`/`model`, validated by arithmetic, degrading to the deterministic word-balanced split on any failure. Each section is planned and validated on its own, then the merged sheet runs the unchanged `validate_beat_sheet` | One call had to emit the entire verbatim-covering sheet, and at doc length (8–12 min) a single dropped sentence fails full coverage for the WHOLE video, three repair attempts over — the failure is global while the mistake is local. Chunking makes failures local and repairable; cutting at even word counts, though, hands a planner half a movement and it plans towards nothing, which is what the spine fixes. Indices rather than echoed script text is the load-bearing choice: the answer is checkable by arithmetic instead of string matching, the call stays cheap however long the script is, and a malformed spine is detectable rather than plausible. It cannot take control (D2) — code decides the sections, splits any the spine made too big, and falls back silently — and it never touches the contract boundary: the merged sheet is judged by exactly the validator a single call was judged by. Whole-video checks (beat-count range, overlay density) are deferred to that merged pass, because each grants a slack term that would otherwise be handed out once per chunk |
| D53 | Beat sheet **v1.1**: a beat may carry `queries[]` — 2–3 short keyword searches — alongside `visual_intent`. Each source declares which it wants (`query_kind`: the library and image generation take the intent, stock takes keywords), keyword sources try the queries in order before the chain falls through, and a v1.0 beat derives keywords from its intent. v1.0 stays valid | One scout sentence was doing three incompatible jobs: a vector query (which wants the whole description), an image-generation prompt (same), and a keyword search (which wants 2–4 words). Pexels matches WORDS — handed "aerial view of a 1940s industrial district, smokestacks, workers assembling aircraft wings" it returns whatever shares "view" and "of", which is how a man at a desk arrived in a wartime documentary. Splitting the field rather than post-processing the sentence keeps the model doing judgment (which two words name this shot) and code doing arithmetic; deriving keywords deterministically when the field is absent is what keeps manual-first true and lets the change land without reprocessing anything. Trying the alternates inside a source, not across sources, follows D12: the chain is ordered by preference, so a second phrasing of the right shot is worth more than the first phrasing at a worse source |
| D54 | A per-video **used-asset ledger**, rebuilt from the plan (not a new artifact), threaded through `resolve_assets`: a source walks its ranked results and skips any asset id this video already used within `source_policy.visual.dedup.reuse_window_items` (default 0 = the whole video), and optionally any candidate closer than `min_hamming_distance` (default 0 = off) to an earlier shot by 64-bit dHash of one frame | Per-beat resolution is independent by design — D12 is a chain per item — and that independence is exactly what makes two adjacent beats about the same subject fetch the same clip, or the same library segment appear three times in eight minutes, which reads as a mistake rather than as a motif. Rebuilding the ledger from the plan rather than keeping a file beside it follows Principle 1: the plan already records every asset's provenance, so a worker killed mid-stage resumes with what it had, and there is no second place for the truth to live. Walking the ranked results instead of failing is what keeps D12 honest — a used segment is worse than the next one down, but still better than falling through to a worse SOURCE. The perceptual check is off by default because it costs one wasted download per rejection and its threshold is a matter of taste per channel; both numbers are channel data, never constants (Principle 4) |
| D55 | Two resolver fallbacks, both channel data: `source_policy.visual.min_score_floor` (default 0 = off) replaces a below-floor match with a plain colour fill plus the style pack's `fallback.component` typographic card, and `short_clip_fallback` (default `[loop, freeze]`) covers a slot longer than its footage — `loop` is a new `visualItem.loop` honoured by BOTH renderers, `slow` ramps playback to at most 0.5x (Remotion only) | A source's `min_score` decided whether to fall THROUGH; nothing decided whether the chain's final answer was worth showing, so a 0.31 match was placed with the same confidence as a 0.85 one. A card naming the subject is honest; a nearly-unrelated clip is a lie the viewer notices and cannot name. Absent `fallback` keeps the weak asset, because a beat with nothing on it is worse than a beat with the wrong thing on it. Short clips were the reverse problem — the two renderers disagreed silently: Remotion froze on the last frame (reads as a stall) and the ffmpeg path simply ran out and truncated the segment. One flag in the plan makes both do the same thing, and it is a plan field rather than renderer cleverness for the usual reason: the editor can see it, a human can turn it off, and it survives a per-beat recompile. The order is config because the right answer is taste — a loop is invisible on a locked-off shot and obvious on a pan |
| D56 | Catalog entries declare a `region` (the vertical band they draw in, as fractions from the top) and the COMPILER decides where each caption sits, writing `bottom_fraction` onto the caption item; an entry with no region is read as full-frame, and the renderer's old blanket rule survives only as the fallback for a plan that carries no answer | Captions and lower thirds want the same strip of frame and nothing reconciled them: the renderer lifted every caption that overlapped ANY component overlay, by a fixed amount, because the plan never said which part of the frame a component draws in. That was wrong in both directions — a centred card moved captions that were never in its way, and a lower third's footer sat exactly where the lifted caption landed. The catalog is the right home for the declaration (it is already the machine-readable description of what a component IS), and the compiler is the right place for the decision, for the same reason every other placement is compiled: deterministic, visible in the plan, editable by hand, identical on both render paths. Clearing a full-frame treatment is impossible by moving the caption, so past `captions.max_lift` the caption steps up by one caption height instead of climbing into the middle of the frame. Remotion-only in effect: the ffmpeg path burns one subtitle style for the whole video, and any component overlay routes to Remotion anyway (D7/D31) |
| D57 | A `qa` STAGE between render and finalize inspects the finished file — sampled frames for black and flat ones, mean/peak level and silence runs for the audio, duration against the voiceover — and fails with one actionable reason; every threshold is channel data under `qa` | Validation was structural: it proved the plan was coherent and the assets existed, which is exactly the evidence a black render, a silent mix or a file cut ninety seconds short all pass with flying colours. The one place that knows the truth is the render itself, and nothing looked at it. Making it a STAGE rather than a report is what makes it a gate: the orchestrator sets RENDERED only after every stage passes, so a broken video stops with a reason instead of arriving in someone's review queue. Sampled frames rather than a full decode keeps it cheap enough to always run, and the flat-frame check (spread across an 8x8 grey sample, not just mean luma) is what catches the failure mean luma cannot see — an overlay painting a panel over the whole shot. The black tolerance exists because a fade_to_black legitimately lands on black, and a check that fails a video for its own fade is a check that gets switched off |
| D58 | Timed beats may sit AFTER the narration as well as before it (amends D28's "leading only"): the contiguous run from 0 is a cold open and delays the voiceover, everything after the first gap is an outro, placed end to end after the last word using its declared DURATION and order rather than its declared start. The planner prompt's editable half explains both with a worked example each | `kind: "timed"` had existed since D28 and was unreachable in practice: nothing explained it to the model, so every video was narration-locked with no way to open on a held image or end on a card over music. Explaining it was the whole of the intended change — until the compiler proved that an outro was impossible, since a timed beat outside the leading run was a hard error. Keeping only the duration and the order of an outro is what makes it authorable at all: its true start is vo_start plus however long the TTS made the narration, a number the planner would have to guess at, and guessing at arithmetic is exactly what Principle 3 forbids. The validator's envelope-collision rule goes with it — collision is now impossible by construction, so what remains is that each timed beat describes a positive span and that two of them do not overlap |
| D59 | A SECOND overlay class: `overlay.emphasis: true` in beat sheet v1.1, enabled per style pack (`overlays.emphasis.enabled`, default false) with its own `per_minute` ceiling counted separately from `overlays.density`. Emphasis overlays are pure-text only — a component with anchor types cannot be one | Overlays are anchor-gated (D8/the welded HARD RULES), so their density tracks the FACTUAL density of the script: a documentary paragraph full of dates and figures earns graphics, and a paragraph carrying the turn of the story earns none, however much attention it deserves. That is right for the doc channel and limiting for a faster one, where the reason to put something on screen is often "this is the line the video exists for". Separate budgets are the load-bearing part: sharing one would let emphasis crowd out the anchor overlays, which is the discipline that makes the first class trustworthy. Off by default, and the number reaches the prompt only when the class is on, so a pack that does not use it composes a byte-identical prompt and validates identically — the change is invisible until a channel asks for it. Pure-text only because a component that carries a fact must get the fact from an anchor, which is the rule emphasis would otherwise quietly undo |
| D60 | The stage list is DATA: a **pipeline manifest** (`contracts/pipelines/<name>.yaml`, schema-validated) declares which stages run, in what order and what each produces; the worker keeps a **step registry** (`name -> body + done-check`) and the orchestrator walks the manifest. Selection is ONE resolver at enqueue, and the chosen manifest is snapshotted into `cfg.json` as `pipeline_doc` | The `STAGES` constant made a second video shape a code change in a Python module, which is the one personalization layer that had never become data (D10/D42 did it for packs and prompts). Splitting POLICY from MECHANISM is the load-bearing part: order and artifacts are the same kind of thing a theme is — a human writes them, comments help, git tracks them — while "done" is not uniform (`script.txt` exists vs. `edit_plan.json` is newer than `beats.json`) and encoding freshness in YAML would be writing code in a config file. So the manifest never names a done-check and the registry never names an order, and a manifest stage with no registry entry fails at LOAD, before any work, when the reason is still 'the manifest and this worker disagree'. `requires` ships advisory — the loop is still ordinal — but validated, so a DAG that does not close is caught in CI rather than mid-video; the alternative (a real dependency scheduler) buys nothing while every pipeline is a line. Selection lives at enqueue in one function because two places that pick a pipeline is two places to disagree, and the snapshot follows the theme/style-pack rule (Principle 7): editing `faceless.yaml` must not change an in-flight video, and a re-run must walk the list it was built with. `faceless.yaml` is the old constant verbatim, pinned by a test, so this change was a refactor with no behavioural difference |
| D61 | A channel declares a **`production_style`** (`faceless`, `talking_head`, `animation`, `shorts`, `ultra_longform`, `custom`) — the same enum as a manifest's `category` — and the D60 resolver turns it into a pipeline name by matching the two: the one `stability: production` manifest of that category wins, `pipeline` still PINS an exact file and overrides it, and a style with no matching manifest is REFUSED at enqueue rather than defaulted | The channel UI had no way to say how a video is made, only what it is (`video_type`), and the only pipeline control anywhere was a raw manifest-name picker on the composer — which asks a human to know file names in order to express a creative decision they make once per channel. Style and file name are not the same lifetime: a family will hold several manifests (a shipped one and a `stability: test` variant), so a channel that pinned a name would have to be re-pointed every time the family's default moved. Reusing `category` rather than inventing a second vocabulary is what keeps the match a lookup instead of a mapping table, and is why adding talking-head production is one .yaml with `category: talking_head` and no code change in the resolver. `video_type` stays orthogonal on purpose: a faceless doc and a talking-head doc are the same content shape produced two different ways, so one picks the style pack and the other picks the pipeline. The refusal is the load-bearing half — silently running `faceless` for a talking-head channel would deliver a wrong video that looks successful, the one failure mode the manifest split (D60) exists to prevent |
| D62 | **Review mode executes** (closes OQ-28). A video carries `checkpoint_policy` (`auto` | `guided`), overriding the manifest's `default_checkpoint_policy`; under `guided` the orchestrator stops after every stage flagged `human_approval_on_review_mode`, sets the video to a new `awaiting_approval` status and returns. An approval is a FILE — `approvals/<stage>.json` in the video folder — and approving re-queues the video. Stages also declare `receivable_on_upload` | The gates had been declared since D60 and executed by nothing, which is the worst of both: a schema that reads like a promise. Putting the policy on the VIDEO rather than in the manifest is what keeps one `faceless.yaml` running both ways — the alternative, a `faceless-review.yaml`, forks every future edit in two and was the exact outcome D60 was written to prevent. The approval is a file for the same reason every other 'is this done?' answer is one: the folder is the data plane of record and resume is 'skip what exists', so a gate needs no new bookkeeping — on the re-claim the artifact is present (stage skips) and the approval is present (gate passes), and the loop carries on with no memory of having stopped. That also makes the pending gate DERIVABLE (the first declared gate with no file), so no column can drift from the truth, and a hand-run video clears a gate with `touch`. The gate fires whether the stage ran or was skipped, which is precisely what makes the re-claim work. `awaiting_approval` is its own status because the orphan sweep re-queues anything stuck in `producing`, and a video parked on a human is not stuck; editors may approve, since reviewing the script and the beat sheet IS the editing job and charging a manager per gate would make review mode cost more than it saves |
| D63 | Subtitle cue size is a CHANNEL field (`transcript.granularity`: `sentence` | `word` | `segment`), not a pipeline field, defaulting to `sentence` | The beats process needs to know what a cue spans, because that is where its script breaks come from — but two channels running the same faceless manifest legitimately want different answers, so a manifest carrying this would have to fork once per answer, which is the same fork D62 refused. `sentence` is the default because it is what every video has produced: the TTS adapters synthesize one sentence at a time and report exact per-sentence timings, so a cue already IS a sentence and the answer costs nothing. `word` is the one answer they cannot give — there is no timing inside a sentence to report — so it always costs an ASR pass, and saying that in the field's own description is what stops it reading as a free toggle.
