In a quiet corner of eastern Pennsylvania, there is a town where the ground has been burning for more than 60 years. Steam still rises from cracks in the soil. The roads lead nowhere. And the town of Centralia, once home to more than 1,000 people, now has just 5.

Nobody set out to destroy it. It began with a routine job that went wrong.

In the late 1800s, Centralia was a busy coal town. Miners dug deep into the hills, following seams of anthracite, the hardest and cleanest-burning coal in America. By 1890, more than 2,500 people lived here. There were churches, hotels, schools and saloons. Beneath their feet, the tunnels spread for miles.

Then, in May 1962, the town council decided to clean up the local dump before Memorial Day. The dump sat in an old strip-mine pit. Most accounts say workers set the trash on fire, as towns often did back then. But the fire did not stay in the pit. It found an opening into the coal seam below, and it slipped underground.

At first, it seemed manageable. Crews dug trenches and poured in water and ash. Nothing worked. The fire kept moving through the old tunnels, feeding on the coal, and pulling in air through every crack.

For almost 20 years, the town lived with it. Basements grew warm. Gas levels were checked in homes. Some residents wanted to leave. Others refused to believe the danger was real.

Everything changed on Valentine's Day, 1981. A 12-year-old boy named Todd Domboski was walking in his grandmother's backyard when the ground opened beneath him. He grabbed onto roots as the hole pulled him down. His cousin reached him in time and dragged him out. The pit was later measured at around 150 feet deep, and the air coming out of it was deadly.

After that, no one could pretend anymore. In 1984, Congress set aside more than 42 million dollars to move the residents out. Most families took the money and left. Houses were torn down one by one, until whole streets were nothing but grass.

In 1992, the state of Pennsylvania took the remaining properties. Ten years later, the Postal Service took away the town's ZIP code. On paper, Centralia had almost stopped existing.

Today, a church still stands on the hill above the empty streets. The old highway, covered in graffiti for years, was buried under dirt in 2020. And below it all, the fire keeps burning.

Experts estimate there is enough coal down there to feed it for another 250 years. Centralia is not the only coal town that was left behind. But it may be the only one where the reason is still burning beneath your feet.
