In 2019, nearly 30 million people took a cruise. And by 2020, that number had collapsed to under 6 million. While Carnival, the largest cruise company on the planet, lost more than $10 billion in a single year and watched its debt explode from roughly $10 billion to almost $35 billion. If you had wanted to bet against an entire industry in the spring of 2020, cruising would have been the easiest bet in the world to make. So here's the part that doesn't make sense. As of the middle of 2026, cruise lines around the world have placed orders for 80 new ships with a total construction bill of roughly $90 billion. And that number has climbed almost every month for over a year, from about 70 billion at the end of 2025 to over 90 billion today. So the industry that looked finished four years ago is now placing the biggest bet on its own future it has ever placed. In 2021, across the entire global cruise industry, exactly one new ship order was placed for the whole year, compared with 2017 and 2018, the two busiest ordering years in the industry's history, when companies were racing each other just to reserve a shipyard slot. The order count went up to eight ships in 2022 and 11 in 2023. That was just a small sign of life rather than a real recovery. And then without much warning, everything changed. People didn't just come back to cruising, they came back in numbers nobody had predicted. In 2025, a record 37.2 million people took a cruise, up from 34.6 million the year before. And the industry's trade group, CLIA, now expects that number to reach 42 million by 2028. Almost 90% of past cruisers say they want to go again, the highest repeat interest figure ever recorded. Nearly a third of last year's passengers had never cruised before at all. It turns out a cruise ship has one advantage a hotel doesn't, which is that it can move. And it can go anywhere on the planet the moment demand shifts. Once travel reopened, that flexibility turned into the industry's greatest strength. So the cruise lines, watching the demand go high, made a collective decision to build and build more than ever before. While every cruise company is ordering new ships, the four biggest companies in the industry are not playing the same game though. And the differences say a lot about each of them. Norwegian Cruise Line currently holds the largest order count of any company, 17 new ships, adding roughly 47,000 berths across its three brands: mainstream Norwegian, midrange Oceania, and ultra luxury Regent Seven Seas. MSC Cruises is playing a different game entirely. In December 2025, it signed a deal with the German shipyard Meyer Werft for four ships plus an option for two more worth about $11.7 billion US. What makes the deal remarkable is that Meyer Werft, a 230-year-old shipyard, had come close enough to bankruptcy a year earlier that the German government had to take an 80% ownership stake just to keep it alive. So, MSC's order didn't only buy six ships, it became just the thing keeping an entire historic shipyard alive.
